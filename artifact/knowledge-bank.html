Stage 0

Foundations: before the Transformer

● You are here pre-2017

Why "attention" had to be invented in the first place.

Language is a sequence, so early neural NLP used recurrent networks (RNNs, and their gated cousins LSTM/GRU): read one token at a time, carrying a hidden state forward that's meant to summarise everything seen so far. Two structural problems fall out of this. First, learning long-range dependencies is hard — gradients vanish or explode across many steps. Second, it's inherently sequential: step t depends on step t−1, so you can't parallelise across the sequence — a hard ceiling on GPUs.

The seq2seq model made it worse by squeezing an entire input sentence into one fixed vector — the information bottleneck. The fix, in 2014, was attention (Bahdanau et al.): instead of one vector, keep all the encoder's per-token states and, at each step, take a learned weighted average of them. That single idea — score every candidate against a query, softmax the scores, return the weighted sum — is the seed of everything that follows.

  • Distributed representations: word2vec / GloVe embeddings — geometry encodes meaning.
  • The bottleneck: one fixed vector can't hold a whole sentence; attention removes it.
  • The pattern to remember: query · score · softmax · weighted-sum of values.

Reference materials

  • paperBahdanau, Cho, Bengio (2014) — Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (attention is born)
  • paperSutskever et al. (2014) — Sequence to Sequence Learning
  • explainerChris Olah — Understanding LSTM Networks
Stage 1

The Transformer

● You are here 2017

"Attention Is All You Need" — throw away recurrence entirely.

The 2017 bet: replace recurrence completely with attention, so the whole sequence is processed in parallel — exactly what GPUs want, and what makes training at massive scale possible. In self-attention, every token is projected into a query, key, and value; the query scores against every key (dot product), a softmax turns scores into weights, and the output is the weighted sum of values — computed for all tokens at once in one matrix multiply.

Multi-head attention runs this several times in parallel subspaces; positional encodings re-inject word order (attention alone is order-blind). Stack these blocks and you get an encoder or a decoder. The one catch that drives Stage 4: self-attention is O(n²) in sequence length.

  • Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ → softmax → ·V: the whole mechanism in one line.
  • Parallelism → scale: no time-stepping means you can train enormous models.
  • Decoder-only wins: it becomes the template for every modern chat LLM.

Reference materials

  • paperVaswani et al. (2017) — Attention Is All You Need
  • explainerJay Alammar — The Illustrated Transformer
  • codeThe Annotated Transformer (Harvard NLP)

Stage 2

The pretraining era

● You are here 2018–2019

Pretrain once on a mountain of text, then transfer everywhere.

The breakthrough insight: train a Transformer on raw, unlabelled text with a self-supervised objective (the text supervises itself), so it learns general language structure — then fine-tune on your task with little labelled data. Two objectives split the field into two families.

GPT (decoder-only) predicts the next token, so it's naturally generative. BERT (encoder-only) fills in masked tokens using both directions, so it's superb at understanding. T5 (encoder–decoder) casts every task as text-in → text-out. The field ultimately bet on decoder-only, because a single next-token objective scales beautifully and absorbs the rest.

  • Self-supervised pretraining → fine-tuning: the recipe still underneath everything.
  • Decoder vs encoder: generate vs understand; causal vs bidirectional.

Reference materials

  • paperDevlin et al. (2018) — BERT
  • paperRadford et al. (2018) — GPT-1 (Generative Pre-Training)
  • paperRaffel et al. (2019) — T5
  • explainerJay Alammar — The Illustrated BERT
Stage 3

Scale and emergence

● You are here 2019–2022

Make the same architecture enormous — and new behaviour appears.

GPT-3 (175B) revealed in-context learning: give a few examples in the prompt and the model does the task with no weight updates. Nobody trained that in explicitly — it emerged from next-token prediction at scale. This is the birth of prompting and of "the model as a general interface."

Scaling laws (Kaplan 2020) made loss a predictable power law of parameters, data, and compute. Chinchilla (2022) then corrected the recipe: earlier models were oversized and under-trained — scale data and parameters together. Data became the scarce resource. "Emergent abilities" is real but contested — some sharp jumps are artefacts of harsh metrics.

  • In-context / few-shot learning: capability with no gradient updates.
  • Chinchilla-optimal: more tokens, not just more parameters.

Reference materials

  • paperBrown et al. (2020) — Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)
  • paperKaplan et al. (2020) — Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models
  • paperHoffmann et al. (2022) — Training Compute-Optimal LLMs (Chinchilla)
  • paperWei et al. (2022) — Emergent Abilities of LLMs
Stage 4

Efficiency & better building blocks

● You are here 2021–2023

Make big Transformers faster, longer, and cheaper to run.

Two costs dominate: attention is O(n²), and dense models run every parameter on every token. FlashAttention attacked the first — it noticed attention is memory-bandwidth bound and computed it tile-by-tile in fast on-chip SRAM without ever materialising the full matrix. Same result, several times faster; it's why long context became affordable.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) attacked the second: many expert MLPs plus a router that sends each token to just a few. Huge total parameters, small active count per token. Modern positional schemes (RoPE, ALiBi) replaced sinusoids and let context windows stretch to hundreds of thousands of tokens. On the serving side: KV cache, GQA, quantisation, LoRA.

  • FlashAttention: exact attention, IO-aware — never materialise the n×n matrix.
  • MoE: decouple total capacity from per-token compute.
  • RoPE / ALiBi: positional schemes that enable long context.

Reference materials

  • paperDao et al. (2022) — FlashAttention
  • paperFedus et al. (2021) — Switch Transformer (MoE)
  • paperSu et al. (2021) — RoFormer / RoPE
  • paperHu et al. (2021) — LoRA
Stage 5

Alignment & post-training

● You are here 2022–now

Turn a next-token predictor into a helpful, honest assistant.

A base model just continues text; it isn't trying to be helpful or safe. Post-training installs that. First SFT — supervised fine-tuning on ideal (instruction, response) pairs. Then RLHF: humans rank model outputs, a reward model learns their preferences, and the LLM is optimised (PPO) to score well — with a KL penalty keeping it from drifting into gibberish. InstructGPT's lesson: a 1.3B aligned model beat 175B GPT-3 on human preference. Alignment, not size.

DPO later showed you can hit the same objective with a simple loss and no RL loop, democratising alignment. Constitutional AI / RLAIF replaces much human labelling with AI feedback guided by written principles. And RLVR — rewarding verifiably correct answers on math/code — is the bridge to Stage 7's reasoning models.

  • SFT → reward model → PPO: the classic RLHF pipeline.
  • DPO: preference optimisation without a separate reward model or RL.
  • Reward hacking & sycophancy: the failure modes to watch.

Reference materials

  • paperOuyang et al. (2022) — InstructGPT
  • paperRafailov et al. (2023) — Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
  • paperBai et al. (2022) — Constitutional AI
  • explainerHugging Face — Illustrating RLHF
Stage 6

Multimodality & generation beyond text

● You are here 2021–now

Give models eyes and ears — and learn to generate pixels.

CLIP trained image and text encoders jointly with a contrastive objective, producing a shared embedding space — you can compare a picture to a sentence directly. That space became the bridge for text-to-image generators and for feeding images into LLMs.

Diffusion is the engine of image generation: gradually add noise to an image, then train a network to reverse one step of noising; generate by denoising from pure noise. Latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion) ran this in a compressed space to make it cheap. Today's frontier models are natively multimodal — text, image, audio in one context.

  • CLIP: contrastive training → a shared image–text space.
  • Diffusion: learn to reverse a noising process.
  • VLMs: a vision encoder projected into an LLM (e.g. LLaVA).

Reference materials

  • paperRadford et al. (2021) — CLIP
  • paperHo et al. (2020) — Denoising Diffusion (DDPM)
  • paperRombach et al. (2021) — Latent / Stable Diffusion
  • explainerLilian Weng — What are Diffusion Models?
Stage 7

Frontier systems & the engineered stack

● You are here 2023 → now

From "a model" to "a system" — retrieval, tools, agents, and reasoning.

The frontier is now defined less by architecture than by post-training and inference-time systems. Long context (128K–1M+ tokens), RAG (retrieve relevant documents at query time to ground answers), and tool use / function calling (let the model call a search engine, a calculator, an API) turn a text predictor into something that can act. An agent is an LLM in a loop: plan → call a tool → observe → repeat.

The defining recent shift is reasoning models (o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1): trained to produce long internal chains of thought and to spend more test-time compute on harder problems, often via RL on verifiable rewards. This reopened rapid progress just as pure-pretraining returns were flattening — a new axis to scale.

  • RAG: ground answers in retrieved documents.
  • Agents: plan → act → observe loops with tools (MCP, function calling).
  • Reasoning models: think longer; RL on verifiable rewards (RLVR).

  • paperLewis et al. (2020) — Retrieval-Augmented Generation
  • explainerLilian Weng — LLM-Powered Autonomous Agents
