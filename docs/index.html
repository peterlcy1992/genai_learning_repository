Foundations: before the Transformer

Why "attention" had to be invented in the first place.

This is your current stage — start here.
🎧

01 The problem: modelling sequences

Language is a sequence, so early neural NLP used recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and their gated successors, LSTM and GRU. An RNN reads one token at a time, updating a hidden state ht that is meant to summarise everything seen so far: ht = f(ht−1, xt).

Two structural problems follow. First, learning long-range dependencies is hard: to connect a word at position 50 to one at position 1, the signal must survive 49 multiplications during backpropagation — so gradients vanish or explode. LSTMs add gates (input, forget, output) and a cell state that flows with light modification, which helps but doesn't eliminate the problem. Second, and just as important, recurrence is inherently sequential: step t needs step t−1, so you cannot parallelise across the sequence — a hard throughput ceiling on GPUs built for parallelism.

02 The mechanism: attention as a learned lookup

The seq2seq model (Sutskever, 2014) uses an encoder RNN to compress the input into a single fixed vector and a decoder RNN to generate from it. The flaw is immediate: one vector must hold an entire sentence — the information bottleneck — and it gets worse the longer the input.

Bahdanau attention (2014) removed it. Keep all the encoder's per-token states. At each decoding step, score the decoder's current state (a query) against every encoder state, turn the scores into weights with a softmax, and return the weighted sum. The decoder now looks back at the whole input and focuses on the relevant parts.

score(q, kᵢ) → αᵢ = softmax(score)ᵢ → context = Σᵢ αᵢ · vᵢ
The one pattern to carry forward. Attention = a content-based, learned, weighted lookup: for a query, score every candidate, softmax the scores into weights, return the weighted average of the values. Stage 1 is this exact operation — just with the RNN thrown away and the sequence attending to itself.

03 The substrate: word embeddings

Underneath all of this sit distributed representations: map each token to a dense vector where geometry encodes meaning (the famous king − man + woman ≈ queen). word2vec (2013) learned these by predicting a word from its neighbours (or vice versa); GloVe (2014) from global co-occurrence counts. These are static — one vector per word regardless of context. Making them contextual is exactly what ELMo and then the Transformer did next.

04 Key concepts

RNN / LSTM
Process a sequence step by step, carrying a hidden state; gates (LSTM) ease but don't fix long-range gradient decay.
seq2seq
Encoder→decoder architecture mapping one sequence to another (translation, summarisation).
Bottleneck
Forcing a whole input into one fixed vector; the flaw attention removes.
Attention
A learned weighted average of candidate values, chosen by scoring them against a query.
Embedding
A dense vector representing a token; geometry captures meaning.

  • Describe the query → score → softmax → weighted-sum pattern in one sentence.
  • What does a word embedding buy you over one-hot symbols?

The Transformer

"Attention Is All You Need" — throw away recurrence entirely.

This is your current stage — start here.
🎧

▶ Listen

01 The bet: attention without recurrence

Recurrence made sequence models slow and forgetful (Stage 0). The 2017 bet was radical: keep the attention, drop the RNN completely. With no time-stepping, the whole sequence is processed in parallel — exactly what GPUs want, and the reason everything after this could be trained at scale. Every advance in this atlas rides on that unlock.

02 Scaled dot-product attention

Each token is linearly projected into three vectors: a query (Q), a key (K), and a value (V). A token's new representation is a weighted average of all tokens' values, where the weights come from how well its query matches each key.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dₖ ) · V

The dot product QKᵀ measures similarity; the √dₖ scaling keeps those products from growing large with dimension and pushing softmax into saturated, tiny-gradient regions; softmax turns scores into weights that sum to one; multiplying by V returns the weighted average. It's the Stage 0 lookup — now every token queries every token, computed in one matrix multiply.

03 From one head to a full block

Multi-head attention runs the operation h times in parallel with different learned projections, then concatenates. Each head can specialise — one tracks syntax, another long-distance coreference — giving several representation subspaces at once.

Positional encodings re-inject order, because attention is permutation-invariant (it has no built-in sense of sequence). The original paper adds fixed sinusoidal vectors of different frequencies; Stage 4's RoPE and ALiBi are the successors that made long context work.

A full Transformer block wraps attention with a position-wise feed-forward network, residual connections (add the input back, so gradients flow), and layer normalisation. Stack N of these and you have an encoder or a decoder.

04 Encoder, decoder, and the O(n²) catch

The original is an encoder–decoder for translation. The encoder reads the source bidirectionally; the decoder generates one token at a time with masked self-attention (a position can't attend to the future — that would leak the answer during next-token training) plus cross-attention into the encoder. This split seeds the three families of Stage 2 — and decoder-only becomes the template for modern LLMs.

The catch that drives Stage 4. Self-attention is O(n²) in sequence length: every token attends to every other, so doubling the context quadruples attention compute and memory. Much of Stage 4 is the long war on that cost.

05 Key concepts

Q / K / V
Per-token query, key, value projections; attention = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)·V.
Multi-head
Several attention operations in parallel subspaces, concatenated.
Positional enc.
Injected order information (sinusoidal → RoPE/ALiBi).
Residual + norm
Add the input back and normalise — lets deep stacks train stably.
Causal mask
Blocks attention to future tokens so decoders can be trained on next-token prediction.

Stage 22018–2019

The pretraining era

Pretrain once on a mountain of text, then transfer everywhere.

This is your current stage — start here.

01 The idea: self-supervision + transfer

Before 2018, most NLP models were trained from scratch on each task's small labelled dataset. The breakthrough: train a Transformer on a massive corpus of raw text using a self-supervised objective — the text supplies its own labels, so no annotation is needed — then fine-tune on your task with comparatively little labelled data. The model arrives already knowing grammar, facts, and patterns. This pretraining → post-training recipe still underlies everything today.

02 Two objectives, three families

GPT (decoder-only) trains on plain language modelling: predict the next token given all previous ones. Because it only ever looks left (causal masking), it's naturally generative — you can sample text from it. This family becomes the backbone of every modern chat LLM.

BERT (encoder-only) uses masked language modelling: hide ~15% of tokens and predict them from context on both sides. That bidirectional view makes it excellent at understanding — classification, retrieval, embeddings — but it doesn't generate. ELMo was the transitional step, producing contextual embeddings from a BiLSTM.

T5 / BART (encoder–decoder) recast every task as text-in → text-out, ideal for translation and summarisation. The field ultimately bet on decoder-only: a single next-token objective scales beautifully and, at scale, absorbs "understanding" tasks too — you can just ask.

03 Tokenization & fine-tuning, briefly

Models don't read characters or whole words but subword tokens (Byte-Pair Encoding for GPT, WordPiece for BERT) — a vocabulary that balances rare-word coverage against sequence length. Fine-tuning then adapts the pretrained weights to a task; a lighter alternative is feature extraction (freeze the model, train a small head on its representations). Stage 5 replaces task fine-tuning with instruction tuning + alignment.

04 Key concepts

Self-supervision
The data provides its own labels (next / masked token) — no human annotation.
Causal LM
Predict the next token (GPT); naturally generative.
Masked LM
Fill in hidden tokens using both sides (BERT); strong at understanding.
Tokenizer
Splits text into subword units (BPE / WordPiece).
Fine-tuning
Further training of a pretrained model on a narrower task.

05 Checkpoints

  • What does "self-supervised pretraining then fine-tuning" mean, and why was it a leap?
  • Contrast GPT's causal objective with BERT's masked objective — what is each best at?
  • Why are decoder-only models naturally generative and encoder-only ones not?
  • Where do encoder–decoder models (T5) fit?

Stage 32019–2022

Scale and emergence

Make the same architecture enormous — and new behaviour appears.

This is your current stage — start here.

01 In-context learning

GPT-2 (1.5B) already showed a good language model could do tasks zero-shot, just from a prompt. GPT-3 (175B) revealed the phenomenon that reoriented the field: in-context learning. Put a few worked examples in the prompt and the model performs the task — with no weight updates.

English: sea otter → loutre de mer  |  English: cheese → ▁▁▁

Nobody trained this in explicitly; it emerged from next-token prediction over diverse data. This is the birth of prompt engineering and of "the model as a general interface."

02 Scaling laws — and the Chinchilla correction

Kaplan et al. (2020) showed test loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data, and compute, over many orders of magnitude. Performance became predictable: you could forecast a bigger model's loss before training it. "Make it bigger" became a funded research programme.

L(C) ≈ (C / Cₒ)^(−α)  —  loss as a power law of compute

Chinchilla (2022) then corrected the recipe. Kaplan-era models were oversized and under-trained. For a fixed compute budget, scale parameters and training tokens roughly together (~20 tokens per parameter) — a 70B model on far more data beat 175B-class models. Data, not just parameters, became the scarce resource.

03 "Emergence" — real, and contested

Wei et al. (2022) catalogued abilities (multi-step arithmetic, some reasoning) that are near-zero in small models and jump sharply above a scale. Then Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued some jumps are artefacts of harsh, discontinuous metrics, not genuine phase transitions. Hold both: scale reliably buys capability, but treat "sudden emergence" claims with a sceptical eye on how the metric was defined.

04 Key concepts

In-context learning
Task performance from in-prompt examples, no gradient updates.
Scaling law
Loss as a power law of parameters, data, and compute.
Chinchilla-optimal
Scale tokens and parameters together (~20:1); data is the bottleneck.
Emergence
Sharp capability jumps with scale — a real observation, a contested framing.

05 Checkpoints

  • What is in-context learning, and why was it surprising?
  • State a scaling law in words — what are its three ingredients?
  • What did Chinchilla change about the optimal parameter-to-data ratio, and why?
  • Why is "emergent abilities" both a real observation and a contested framing?

Stage 42021–2023

Efficiency & better building blocks

Make big Transformers faster, longer, and cheaper to run.

This is your current stage — start here.

01 Two costs to beat

Scaling ran into two bills. Attention is O(n²) in sequence length, and dense models run every parameter on every token. This whole stage is the field's answer to those two costs — the engineering that turned "a model" into an engineered system.

02 FlashAttention: exact, but IO-aware

The naïve view treats attention as compute-bound. FlashAttention (2022) noticed it's actually memory-bandwidth bound — the bottleneck is shuttling the big n×n attention matrix in and out of GPU high-bandwidth memory. Its fix computes attention tile-by-tile in fast on-chip SRAM, using an online softmax so the full matrix is never materialised. Same exact result, several times faster, far less memory. It's why long context became affordable, and it's baked into every modern training stack.

03 Mixture-of-Experts: capacity ≠ compute

A dense model uses all its parameters for every token. Mixture-of-Experts replaces the feed-forward layer with many expert MLPs plus a router that sends each token to only a few (say 2 of 64). The result: a huge total parameter count with a small active count per token — more capacity without proportional compute.

The lineage runs GShard → Switch Transformer → today's frontier: Mixtral made open MoE mainstream, and DeepSeek-V3 and most current frontier models are large MoEs. The trade-offs: harder training (load balancing, routing collapse) and more memory to hold all experts — but far cheaper inference per unit of quality.

04 Long context & cheap serving

Sinusoidal positions don't extrapolate past training length. RoPE encodes position by rotating Q/K vectors by an angle proportional to position — relative by construction and extendable (position interpolation, YaRN). ALiBi adds a distance bias to attention scores. Together they took context from 512 tokens to hundreds of thousands.

Serving is its own discipline: the KV cache stores past keys/values so generation is O(n) per token (its size is now a primary memory constraint); MQA/GQA shrink it; quantisation (INT8/INT4), speculative decoding, and PagedAttention cut cost further. LoRA/QLoRA adapt giant models by training tiny low-rank add-ons.

05 Key concepts

FlashAttention
Exact attention computed in SRAM tiles; never materialises the n×n matrix.
MoE
Many experts + a router; big total, small active parameters per token.
RoPE / ALiBi
Positional schemes that extrapolate to long context.
KV cache · GQA
Cache past keys/values (O(n) generation); share them across heads to shrink it.
LoRA
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning via small low-rank adapters.

06 Checkpoints

  • Why is attention O(n²), and what are the two families of responses?
  • State FlashAttention's core insight in one sentence.
  • What does MoE change — total vs active parameters — and what does it cost?
  • Why did RoPE/ALiBi replace sinusoids, and what is the KV cache?

Stage 52022 → now

Alignment & post-training

Turn a next-token predictor into a helpful, honest assistant.

This is your current stage — start here.

01 Why a base model isn't an assistant

A base LLM is trained to continue internet text. Ask it a question and it might continue with more questions, because that's a plausible web continuation. It was never optimised to be helpful, truthful, or safe. Post-training installs those behaviours — and it's where most of the perceived progress of the ChatGPT era came from.

02 RLHF: SFT → reward model → PPO

The landmark is InstructGPT (2022), the direct ancestor of ChatGPT. Three steps: (1) SFT — supervised fine-tuning on ideal (instruction, response) pairs, teaching the habit of being an assistant. (2) Reward model — humans rank competing responses; a model learns to predict those preferences as a scalar. (3) RL — optimise the LLM (PPO) to score highly under the reward model, with a KL penalty leashing it to the SFT model so it doesn't reward-hack into gibberish.

The lesson of the stage. A 1.3B RLHF model was preferred by humans over the 175B base GPT-3. Alignment, not size, made it useful.

03 DPO, Constitutional AI, and the bridge to reasoning

RLHF with PPO is finicky. DPO (2023) showed you can hit the same preference objective with a simple classification-style loss directly on preference pairs — no reward model, no RL loop — which democratised alignment for open models. Constitutional AI / RLAIF (Anthropic) replaces much human labelling with AI feedback guided by a written set of principles, scaling oversight and making behaviour auditable.

And RLVR — RL from verifiable rewards — rewards correct answers on checkable tasks (math, code) directly, skipping the human preference step entirely. That is the engine of Stage 7's reasoning models.

Failure modes to watch. Reward hacking (satisfying the reward's letter, not its intent) and sycophancy (telling you what you want to hear) are central, recurring alignment problems — not solved, only managed.

04 Key concepts

SFT
Supervised fine-tuning on ideal instruction–response pairs.
Reward model
Predicts human preference as a scalar, trained on rankings.
PPO + KL
RL that optimises the reward while staying near the reference model.
DPO
Preference optimisation with a simple loss — no reward model or RL loop.
RLVR
Reward verifiably-correct answers (math/code); the reasoning-model engine.

05 Checkpoints

  • Why isn't a base model a helpful assistant, and what does SFT fix?
  • Walk through the three RLHF steps and the role of the KL penalty.
  • Why does the "1.3B beats 175B" InstructGPT result matter?
  • What does DPO remove from RLHF, and what do Constitutional AI / RLVR replace?

Stage 62021 → now

Multimodality & generation beyond text

Give models eyes and ears — and learn to generate pixels.

This is your current stage — start here.

01 CLIP: a shared image–text space

CLIP (2021) trained an image encoder and a text encoder jointly on 400M (image, caption) pairs with a contrastive objective: pull matching image–text pairs together in embedding space and push mismatched pairs apart. The payoff is a shared embedding space — you can compare a picture to a sentence directly, do zero-shot classification ("a photo of a cat" vs "a photo of a dog"), and — crucially — use it to condition image generators and to feed images into LLMs.

02 Diffusion: learn to reverse noise

The dominant image-generation paradigm is diffusion. Forward: gradually add Gaussian noise to an image over many steps until it's pure noise. Reverse: train a network (a U-Net, later a Transformer) to undo one step of noising — to predict and remove a little noise. Generate by starting from pure noise and running the learned denoiser step by step.

Latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion, 2021) made this cheap by running the process in a compressed latent space and conditioning on text via cross-attention to a CLIP encoder. Classifier-free guidance strengthens prompt adherence; DiT swapped the U-Net for a Transformer, the basis of today's high-end image and video systems.

03 Multimodal LLMs

The frontier trend bolts perception onto a strong LLM. Flamingo and BLIP-2 fed visual features into a frozen LLM via lightweight bridges; LLaVA popularised a simple open recipe (vision encoder → projection → LLM, instruction-tuned). Today's frontier models (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini) are natively multimodal — image, and increasingly audio/video, in the same context as text. The mental model: encode each modality into tokens in a shared space, then let the Transformer attend across all of them. CLIP-style alignment is what makes those spaces compatible.

04 Key concepts

CLIP
Contrastive training of image + text encoders into one shared space.
Diffusion
Generate by learning to reverse a gradual noising process.
Latent diffusion
Run diffusion in a compressed latent space (Stable Diffusion).
CF guidance
Strengthens how strongly a diffusion sample follows the prompt.
VLM
An LLM given a vision encoder (e.g. LLaVA); natively-multimodal at the frontier.

05 Checkpoints

  • What is CLIP's objective, and why is a shared image–text space so useful?
  • Describe the forward and reverse processes of a diffusion model.
  • What did latent diffusion change to make Stable Diffusion practical?
  • How does a VLM like LLaVA connect an image encoder to an LLM?

Stage 72023 → now

Frontier systems & the engineered stack

From "a model" to "a system" — retrieval, tools, agents, and reasoning.

This is your current stage — the digests take over from here.

01 The frontier is post-training + inference systems

The common architecture is still a decoder-only Transformer — now usually a large MoE, multimodal, aligned, long-context. What defines the frontier are the deltas in post-training and inference-time systems, not new attention math.

02 Long context, RAG, tools

Long context (128K–1M+ tokens) lets whole codebases or books fit in one prompt — though "lost in the middle" means it complements rather than replaces retrieval. RAG retrieves relevant documents at query time (embed the corpus into a vector DB, fetch nearest chunks, feed them in) to ground answers with fresh, citable knowledge.

Tool use / function calling lets the model emit a structured call — search, calculator, code interpreter, an API — read the result, and continue. MCP emerged as an open standard for connecting models to tools and data. Tool use turns a text predictor into something that can act — the foundation of agents.

03 The reasoning-model shift

Through ~2023, progress came mostly from scaling pretraining. The 2024–2025 shift moved a major axis to inference-time compute. OpenAI o1/o3 are trained to produce long internal chains of thought and to spend more test-time compute on harder problems; accuracy on math/coding/science jumped sharply. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) showed openly that large-scale RL on verifiable rewards (with formats like GRPO) can induce self-checking and backtracking with minimal supervised data.

The new scaling story. "Think longer" — test-time / inference-time scaling — is a distinct lever from pretraining scale, and it reopened rapid progress just as pretraining returns were flattening.

04 Cross-cutting themes to watch

Efficiency & small models (distillation, quantisation, strong on-device models); data as the scarce resource (synthetic data, quality, filtering); evaluation (benchmarks saturate fast: MMLU → GPQA → frontier math and agentic evals like SWE-bench — "evals are the new unit tests"); and safety & alignment at scale (interpretability, oversight, jailbreak robustness, governance).

05 Key concepts

RAG
Retrieve relevant documents at query time to ground answers.
Tool use / MCP
Structured calls to external functions; MCP is the open connection standard.
Agent
An LLM in a plan → act → observe loop with tools and some autonomy.
Reasoning model
Trained to think at length before answering (o1/o3, R1).
Test-time scaling
Spend more compute at inference to improve answers (RLVR-trained).

06 Checkpoints

  • Why is the frontier now defined more by post-training + inference than by architecture?
  • What is RAG, and when would you reach for it vs long context vs fine-tuning?
  • How do tool use and the agent loop turn an LLM into something that acts?
  • What changed with reasoning models — test-time compute and RLVR — and why did R1 matter?

Staying current: the refresh loop

Turn the daily firehose into a few durable improvements — the stage that never ends.

This is your current stage — a loop you run, not a page you finish.

01 Why this stage exists

A plan that stops at Stage 7 rots within months; trying to read everything burns you out and teaches nothing durable, because you never stop to integrate. Stage 8 is the middle path — a repeatable triage that spends your scarce attention on the few developments that change how you'd build or reason, and lets the rest wash past.

The mantra. Most news is noise; a little of it is a genuine improvement. Your job is to find that little — and actually absorb it.

02 The refresh loop

Skim (daily). The digest already did first-pass filtering — read the one-line "why it matters"; open nothing yet unless it's obviously seismic.

Triage (weekly). Re-read the week's digests together and pick the 1–3 most critical items (rubric below). A week's remove from the hype makes the signal far easier to see.

Integrate (weekly). Read the primary source, then write the change down — a line in the right note and a sentence on what it changes. If it changes nothing, it wasn't critical.

Prune (monthly). Mark what's been superseded. A current bank beats a complete one; additive-only notes decay into a museum.

03 What counts as "critical" — the rubric

Score a candidate on four axes; integrate the ones that score high on most:

Durability — paradigm shift or increment? "Will this still matter in a year?" Breadth — how many downstream things does it change (a training recipe beats one model release). Evidence — reproduced, open, measured beats a press demo; discount hype, wait for replication. Proximity — does it touch your goals and stage?

The test. Would you change a sentence in the knowledge bank because of it? If yes, it's probably critical. If it's "another model, slightly better," it's log-and-move-on.

04 Scout vs. curator

The daily Routine is your scout, not your curator: it searches, de-dupes, links, tags each find with a "Relates to: Stage N," and archives a digest. Stage 8 is the human-in-the-loop step on top — the machine ranks recency and relevance, but only you decide what durably updates your model of the field. Let the job gather; you curate.

05 Habits that make it stick

Small & regular
A 30-minute weekly pass sustains for years; a monthly binge doesn't.
